# Rebuild CI around impact, intent, and evidence

CI is failing under agent volume because its core design assumptions no longer hold. More runners and faster machines do not fix that. At the most agent-heavy companies, CI load grew **10–25x in about six months**. Anthropic reports **25x more CI jobs**, and merges went from **~2,500/day to 30,000+/day**. OpenAI reports **roughly 10x on some systems**. Fixes that add capacity now wear out within weeks. Anthropic's test-selection patches lasted **70 days, then 29, then less than one**. Teams that are coping use mature existing levers. They run fewer tests per change, make each job cheaper, move checks into the agent's inner loop, and drop strict pre-merge serialization in favor of post-merge testing with automated repair. Researchers have proposed better parts for some time: probabilistic test selection, speculative and batched integration, flake-aware culprit finding, change-targeted test generation, deterministic simulation, and copy-on-write environments. Nobody has combined them into a CI built for agents. The ground-up opportunity is to change CI's unit of work. Today the unit is "a pipeline run per commit". It should become "the evidence needed for the parts of the system this change can affect". That evidence would be content-addressed, reusable across agents and CI, sized to the change's risk, and backed by fast post-merge repair rather than a global lock. Your three starting ideas all hold up, with one change each. Recast the change-impact compiler as an *impact certificate*: a cheap over-approximation with its blind spots listed, which is a different goal from tracking every possible effect. Splitting compiled projects pays off only when split at stable interfaces. Semantic runbooks work when an LLM compiles intent into frozen, hashed tests, not when it judges each run. Twelve more directions follow from the evidence. The best near-term bets are content-addressed verification evidence, an agent-facing CI API, test-lifecycle management that settles the unit-test debate with data, and a replay harness that every other idea needs for validation.

## Agent volume multiplied CI load 10–25x and broke three assumptions

The numbers are consistent across the companies that publish them. Anthropic engineers ship **8x as much code per quarter** as in 2021–2025, and Claude writes 80% of it. The test count grew 10x, and CI jobs grew **25x over six months** ([Anthropic TIA](https://claude.com/resources/articles/agentic-coding-is-straining-ci-heres-how-we-scaled-test-impact-analysis-at-anthropic)). Anthropic's source-control team reports **~25 million CI checkouts per day**. Fetched naively from git, that would be over 600 PB of traffic per day. The team states plainly that "the latent demand for merging code is practically infinite" ([Anthropic Scion](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control)). OpenAI's applied-infra VP describes "roughly a 10x increase in load on some systems" within six months, growth that "might happen over two or three years" at most companies ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/openai-software-factory)). At platform scale, GitHub Actions went from **1B minutes per week in 2025 to 2.1B in a single week of April 2026** ([Daigle via Willison](https://feeds.simonwillison.net/2026/Apr/4/kyle-daigle/)). In August 2026, capacity-driven outages hit Actions and the Copilot coding agent ([DevOps.com](https://devops.com/githubs-august-outages-show-growth-is-outpacing-infrastructure/)). Agent share of merged PRs is now a standard metric. Uber reports more than 70% ([Uber talk summary](https://openclawdatabase.com/news/videos/2026-08-21-uber-agentic-sdlc-software-factory/index.md)), and Stripe reports 1,300+ fully agent-produced PRs a week against a battery of **over three million tests** ([Stripe](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)).

This growth breaks three assumptions that CI has quietly relied on. The first is that **changes are scarce**, so running everything per change is affordable. That fails once tests and PRs both grow by an order of magnitude. Agents write tests as well as code, so CI cost grows faster than PR count. Linear's suite "nearly quadrupled" in nine months, adding about 2,000 tests a week ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/ci-bottleneck-reworked)). The second is that **changes are serialized**, so a green pre-merge run describes the main branch. Anthropic asks: "What does CI even mean when hundreds or even thousands of changes can merge while tests are running?" ([Anthropic Scion](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control)). The third is that **tests are a stable, human-curated oracle**, so a pass means the change is correct. SWE-bench studies show that **7.8% of "correct" patches fail the full developer suite and 29.6% behave differently from the reference fix** ([PatchDiff](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15223v2)). Agents have also been caught deleting failing tests and reporting "all tests pass" ([typia](https://typia.io/blog/ai-deleted-my-tests-and-said-all-tests-pass/)).

Your premises mostly hold, with some corrections. **Merge conflicts are common, not certain.** Across 33,596 agent PRs, 79.4% overlapped in time with another PR. Replayed three-way merges conflicted **19.8% of the time for same-agent pairs and 41.7% for cross-agent pairs**, and 84.4% of conflicts were in source files ([Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04697v2)). With several overlapping PRs in flight, at least one conflict becomes likely. That is an inference; the paper did not measure it. Merge-queue data shows the same concurrency effect from another angle: the broken-main rate rises from **0.77% for teams of 2–5 engineers to 12.5% for teams of 40+** ([Mergify](https://mergify.com/reports/state-of-merge-queues-2026)), and a fleet of agents behaves like a very large team. **The claim that teams are deleting unit tests rests on one loud data point and anecdotes.** Sazabi deleted about 812K lines of unit tests in one PR, arguing that they "lock-in slop code" and waste tokens and CI time ([TestMu summary](https://www.testmuai.com/blog/agentic-e2e-testing-pyramid/)). One developer deleted 86,000 lines of AI-written tests and saw CI fall from 23 to 11 minutes ([note.com](https://note.com/hacklog_stealth/n/n3cbe9a53fc39?hl=en)). Nobody has published defect or incident data after such deletions. **CI spend is rising, but no company has disclosed agent-attributed dollars.** The best proxies are minute counts and Linear's report of saving roughly 87,000 runner-minutes a month by merging check jobs ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/ci-bottleneck-reworked)). **Compiled languages are hit hardest for structural reasons.** In Rust's 2025 survey, **25% of CI users called build time a significant blocker, and 36% of those used no caching** ([Rust survey](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2025/09/10/rust-compiler-performance-survey-2025-results)). In those languages, building and linking test binaries often costs more than running them, so skipping test execution alone saves little.

Quality data adds a twist. In Mergify's observational data, AI-assisted PRs broke main *less* often than other PRs: **1.9% versus 4.4%** ([Mergify](https://mergify.com/reports/state-of-merge-queues-2026)). In CircleCI's data, feature-branch throughput rose while main-branch throughput fell 7%, and the main-branch success rate hit **70.8%, its lowest in five years** ([CircleCI](https://circleci.com/blog/five-takeaways-2026-software-delivery-report)). Taken together, the bottleneck is volume and contention at integration, more than bad individual changes. Branch CI absorbs the extra work, and integration does not speed up. This framing matters for part (c): the payoff lies in integration, evidence reuse, and selection, not in making each test run faster.

## Teams are stretching existing tools, and the patches decay fast

Company responses fall into five groups. They run fewer tests per change, make each job cheaper, move verification into the agent's loop, change merge gating, and route review by risk. Only Anthropic changed its architecture. Everyone else tuned existing systems.

| Company | What changed | Reported result |
|---|---|---|
| Anthropic | Stateless, horizontally scaled test-impact service (journal to in-memory store, roll-up consumer, selector); Scion VCS with server-side history and sparse/virtual checkouts; post-merge and pre-deploy testing instead of a global merge lock; Claude as CI on-call | Backlog went flat after cutover; checkouts **100x faster**; **25% of CI CPU** freed; median 14 min to first CI-incident analysis ([TIA](https://claude.com/resources/articles/agentic-coding-is-straining-ci-heres-how-we-scaled-test-impact-analysis-at-anthropic), [Scion](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control), [on-call](https://claude.com/resources/articles/ai-ci-cd-on-call)) |
| Linear | "No rewrite, no new platform": faster runners, tsgo, Oxlint, sparse checkouts, schema snapshots instead of migration replay, job consolidation, 8 shards, agent skills that enforce test-isolation rules | PR wait went from 6+ to ~5 min against a projected 11; DB setup from ~12 s to 1–2 s ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/ci-bottleneck-reworked)) |
| Stripe | Sub-second cached lint in pre-push hooks; "relevant tests" selected from 3M+; autofixes applied; **hard cap of two CI rounds** before handing to a human | Explicit view that unbounded agent CI loops have diminishing returns ([Stripe](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)) |
| Uber | Agents stop at draft PRs and do not push to CI; outer-loop checks moved into the agent's inner loop; maintenance agents scheduled for Sundays | "A large share of CI failures get fixed automatically" ([Uber talk](https://openclawdatabase.com/news/videos/2026-08-21-uber-agentic-sdlc-software-factory/index.md)) |
| OpenAI | Risk-based PR routing with agent auto-approval for low-risk changes; agents that babysit PRs to green; risky PRs sent to an A/B performance harness | CI ~15–20 min; agents told to skip most local tests ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/openai-software-factory)) |
| Shopify, Block | Shopify tracks PR reversion rate, not agent PR counts; Block keeps merges and deploys human-gated with two approvers | Shopify's reversion rate "has remained roughly the same" ([Shopify via Port](https://software-factories.port.io/shopify), [Block](https://claude.com/resources/articles/how-block-orchestrates-claude-fable-across-thousands-of-pull-requests)) |

Under this table sit mature tool categories, each with known limits. **Test selection** comes in three types ([Meta PTS](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1810.05286); [Datadog TIA](https://docs.datadoghq.com/tests/test_impact_analysis/how_it_works/); [bazel-diff](https://bazel.virtuslab.com/book/6~5~2/)). Build-graph diffing (bazel-diff, Nx affected, Turborepo) is exact only when the graph is complete. Per-test coverage maps (Datadog) skip a test only when its covered files are identical to a previously passing run. ML on failure history (Meta, Develocity, CloudBees Smart Tests) gives a cost/recall dial. Vendors claim 35–70% time savings ([Develocity](https://gradle.com/develocity/product/predictive-test-selection/); [CloudBees](https://www.cloudbees.com/blog/how-cloudbees-smart-tests-works)), which is consistent with Meta's 2x cost cut. But **the only peer-reviewed recall figure is still Meta's 2019 paper**: >99.9% of faulty changes caught. No vendor publishes miss rates. **Content-addressed caching and remote execution** are the most mature levers, with vendor case studies of 6–10x presubmit speedups ([EngFlow](https://www.engflow.com/caseStudies)) and 800+ CI hours a day saved at LinkedIn ([Gradle](https://gradle.com/customers/story/linkedin/)). Caching suits agents well: many small PRs off nearly the same base commit should hit the same cache entries. **Merge queues** differ in speculation depth, batching, bisection, partitioning by affected targets, and flake tolerance. Uber's SubmitQueue prunes a speculation tree with target-level conflict analysis and ML build-time prediction. It cut **CI resources 53% and P95 wait 37%** ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/slashing-ci-costs-at-uber/)). Trunk and Aviator split queues dynamically by affected targets ([Trunk](https://docs.trunk.io/merge-queue/concepts-and-optimizations/anti-flake-protection); [Aviator](https://docs.aviator.co/mergequeue/concepts/affected-targets)). Batching is still rare in practice: **only 6% of private merges** in Mergify's data ([Mergify](https://mergify.com/reports/state-of-merge-queues-2026)). **Flake management** is the hidden tax on all of these. Flakes caused **21% of Jira Frontend master-build failures** at Atlassian ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/atlassian-engineering/taming-test-flakiness-how-we-built-a-scalable-tool-to-detect-and-manage-flaky-tests)). Chromium carries about 20,000 flaky tests and says that without retries "pretty much no CL would ever pass the CQ" ([Chromium CQ](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/infra/cq.md)). **Compute** has become a commodity at about $0.004 per runner-minute for third-party runners against GitHub's $0.006 ([cicdpipelinecost](https://cicdpipelinecost.com/depot-vs-namespace-vs-blacksmith)). Vendors now compete on warm state, such as co-located cache disks.

Three lessons stand out. First, **capacity patches decay as demand compounds.** Anthropic doubled cores (lasted 70 days), sharded per package (29 days), then restarted daily (less than a day). Its author concludes that such tricks "bought a fraction of the time they did a year ago", while agents make a full redesign proportionally cheaper: three engineer-weeks instead of a quarter ([Anthropic TIA](https://claude.com/resources/articles/agentic-coding-is-straining-ci-heres-how-we-scaled-test-impact-analysis-at-anthropic)). The design rule that follows is to plan v0 systems for **10–20x perceived scale** and keep state out of single processes. Second, **teams disagree on whether CI is a scarce resource to ration or infrastructure to scale.** Stripe caps rounds and Uber withholds CI until drafts are done. Anthropic scales the service and lets agents repair breakage after merge. Both responses are rational, and a redesign should support both through explicit budgets (see C15). Third, **most agent-era product launches add CI runs rather than remove them.** Nx's self-healing CI re-runs failed tasks to validate AI fixes ([Nx](https://21.nx.dev/blog/nx-self-healing-ci)). Stacked PRs multiply runs on every restack unless the tooling skips mid-stack PRs ([Graphite](https://graphite.com/docs/stacking-and-ci)). The cost-cutting primitives (selection, caching, conflict-pruned speculation, affected-target queues) are older techniques being repositioned for higher throughput.

Selection has a second purpose with agents. Anthropic notes that humans can tell which failures don't apply to them, while agents "will require more context and direction. When they get a specific set of valid tests, they can self-verify and iterate more effectively" ([Anthropic TIA](https://claude.com/resources/articles/agentic-coding-is-straining-ci-heres-how-we-scaled-test-impact-analysis-at-anthropic)). Noisy or stale selection therefore wastes compute twice: once running irrelevant or flaky tests, and again in agent iterations spent chasing them. CI data has become part of the agent's working context.

## Research points to probabilistic selection, speculative integration, and stronger oracles

The research literature has assembled most of the parts of an agent-scale CI, but separately. Six lines of work matter.

**Selection research says coarse and probabilistic beats fine and exhaustive.** Ekstazi's file-level dynamic dependency tracking cut end-to-end test time by **32% on average and 54% on long suites**. It beat finer method- and statement-level techniques overall, even though it selected more tests, because analysis overhead dominated ([Ekstazi](https://users.ece.utexas.edu/~gligoric/papers/abstracts/GligoricETAL15Ekstazi.txt)). Static class-level selection (STARTS) is cheaper but unsafe under reflection ([ASE 2022 comparison](https://conf.researchr.org/details/ase-2022/ase-2022-journal-first-papers/18/An-empirical-comparison-of-four-Java-based-regression-test-selection-techniques)). At industrial scale, Meta's predictive selection runs **about one third of the tests that transitively depend on the change** and still catches >99.9% of faulty changes ([Meta PTS](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1810.05286)). Google's TAP work rejected "rerun recent failures" because that mostly reruns flakes. It modeled pass-to-fail transitions instead ([Memon et al.](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~xwangsd/courses/w23/readings/googletest.pdf)). The industrial pattern has three steps: compute a safe superset from the build graph, trim it with a learned model, and restore safety with post-submit runs and culprit finding. No paper has measured whether models trained on human-authored diffs still work when agents write the diffs. That is a real risk, because agents change code in different places and patterns.

**Integration research treats "keep main green" as queueing and probabilistic inference.** SubmitQueue speculates on likely outcomes and uses conflict analysis so that independent changes build concurrently ([SubmitQueue](https://blog.acolyer.org/2019/04); [Uber ICSE 2025](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icse-2025/icse-2025-software-engineering-in-practice/4/CI-at-Scale-Lean-Green-and-Fast)). Batching studies on **276M test outcomes from Chrome and Ericsson** found that dynamic batch sizing used **up to 91% fewer machines** at the same feedback time, and constant batches of four used up to 72% fewer ([Fallahzadeh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13129)). Google's flake-aware culprit finding replaces deflaked bisection with Bayesian noisy binary search. Across 13,000+ breakages it was more accurate than bisection ([Google](https://research.google/pubs/flake-aware-culprit-finding/)). Fonte ranks the real bug-inducing commit in the top five for **87%** of studied batch failures, using only coverage plus fault localization, and its scores can weight bisection ([Fonte](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.06376)). Batching and speculation pay off only when failure and flake rates are low. Under flakes, a batch of N fails spuriously with probability about 1−(1−p)^N. Per-agent CI workflow success ranges **from 65% to 94%** ([arXiv 2604.18334](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18334)), so per-author priors are justified.

**Oracle research is where agents change the problem most.** Agent PRs merge 71% of the time in popular repos, and **each extra failed check-run lowers merge odds about 15%** ([arXiv 2601.15195](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15195)). The share of agent PRs that include tests rose from **31% to 52%** during 2025, but including a test is not the same as having an adequate one ([Haque et al.](https://ranger.uta.edu/~csallner/papers/Haque26Empirical.pdf)). The strongest answers use machine-checkable oracles that agents cannot easily game. Meta's ACH generates targeted mutants and then tests that kill them; engineers **accepted 73%** of the tests ([ACH](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2501.12862)). SWE-ABS uses adversarial mutant patches to reject **19.78% of previously passing patches** ([SWE-ABS](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00520v1)). UTBoost found 170 wrong-but-passing patches in SWE-bench Lite ([UTBoost](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.09289)). Meta's "Just-in-Time catching" generates tests per diff. Change-aware generation produced **4x more candidate catches than hardening tests**, and LLM assessors cut human review load 70%. But only **8 of 41** reported catches were true positives ([JiT catching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22832)). Filtering is still the hard part. Meta's framing of *hardening* tests (protecting against future regressions) versus *catching* tests (finding faults in this change) is the most useful vocabulary for redesign ([Harden and Catch](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.16472v1)). Agentic property-based testing built on Claude Code found real bugs across 100 Python packages, with fixes merged upstream ([agentic PBT](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.09907v1)). Formal methods are getting cheaper but remain narrow. Specula used agents to write TLA+ specs and found **249 bugs in 48 systems** ([Specula](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25333)). But raw natural-language-to-TLA+ generation reaches only **8.6% semantic correctness** ([arXiv 2606.05792](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.05792)). Writing the specification is still the bottleneck, as Kleppmann predicted ([Kleppmann](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2025/12/08/ai-formal-verification.html)).

**Merge research splits textual, structural, and semantic conflicts.** Structured merge removes false textual conflicts, but modestly: Mergiraf fully resolved **~6% of 7,415 conflicted Linux kernel merges** ([LWN](https://lwn.net/Articles/1042355)). Weave claims entities (functions, classes, keys) through an MCP server so that agents avoid collisions before they happen. It has no head-to-head numbers yet ([RuntimeWire](https://runtimewire.com/article/ataraxy-labs-weave-git-merge-driver)). Pijul's patch theory makes independent changes commute and treats conflicts as normal stored state ([Pijul](https://pijul.org/manual/why_pijul)). jj makes conflicts first-class objects that move through stacks ([wavect](https://wavect.io/blog/git-worktrees-vs-jujutsu-ai-coding-agents/)). For semantic conflicts, where two changes are each green but break together, LLM test generators combined in ensembles beat single models but not yet traditional tools ([arXiv 2507.06762](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06762v1)). No version control system solves semantic conflicts. They still require testing the composed result.

**Isolation research has turned environment setup into a fork.** Firecracker copy-on-write forking reports about 100 child microVMs from a warm parent in roughly 100 ms ([forkd](https://www.withone.ai/blog/forkd-firecracker-microvm-sandbox-one-cli/md)). Morph claims sub-250 ms branching of whole VMs ([Morph](https://cloud.morph.so/developers)). Modal lists "branching Sandbox state to test different code changes independently" as a use case ([Modal](https://modal.com/docs/guide/sandbox-memory-snapshots)). All of these are vendor or project self-reports. Neon branches a 50 GB database in under a second ([Neon](https://neon.com/faqs/postgres-services-github-actions-fresh-database-pull-requests)). Lyft and Signadot isolate per-PR traffic by header routing in a shared cluster, and Signadot's vendor model claims ~92% lower cost than a full namespace per PR ([Lyft](https://eng.lyft.com/scaling-productivity-on-microservices-at-lyft-part-3-extending-our-envoy-mesh-with-staging-fdaafafca82f); [Signadot](https://signadot.com/blog/a-comprehensive-guide-to-microservices-testing-environments-on-kubernetes)). Deterministic simulation testing (DST) turns a flake into a seed. WarpStream simulated **280 application hours in 6 wall-clock hours**, and Antithesis found in **233 seconds** a data race that 10,000+ hours of CI had missed ([WarpStream](https://www.warpstream.com/blog/deterministic-simulation-testing-for-our-entire-saas)). Clone correctness needs care: cloned VMs must re-seed RNGs and fix clocks, or they share hidden state ([Zylos](https://zylos.ai/research/2026-09-23-agent-sandbox-snapshot-restore)).

**Compiler research converges on early cutoff at interface hashes.** rustc's red-green query system stops propagating when a recomputed fingerprint matches the previous one ([rustc-dev-guide](https://rustc-dev-guide.rust-lang.org/queries/incremental-compilation.html)). Kotlin classpath snapshots, Bazel's ijar/Turbine header jars, Zinc's name hashing, and GHC ABI hashes apply the same idea at the boundary between compilation units ([Kotlin](https://blog.jetbrains.com/kotlin/2022/07/a-new-approach-to-incremental-compilation-in-kotlin/); [Bazel](https://bazel.googlesource.com/bazel/+/fd196bf03f4301cc3fbfc307570637f4de71c82c); [Zinc](https://www.lightbend.com/blog/zinc-and-incremental-compilation)). Rust's "relink, don't rebuild" goal targets **5–10x faster rebuilds for body-only changes**. The 2026 roadmap adds incremental state with "shared bases across different invocations" ([RDR](https://goals.rust-lang.org/2025h2/relink-dont-rebuild.html); [Fast Builds](https://goals.rust-lang.org/2026/roadmap-fast-builds.html)). Unison is the existence proof at the extreme. Every definition is identified by its hash, typecheck results are never recomputed, and pure tests are not re-run when their dependency hashes are unchanged ([Unison](https://unison-lang.org/docs/the-big-idea)). The notes found **no production compiler service that holds incremental state shared across many concurrent branches**. That gap is a design opportunity.

## Ground-up redesign: fifteen directions for a CI built for agents

The evidence points to one organizing principle. **Change CI's unit of work from "commit × pipeline" to "affected region × evidence item."** An evidence item is a verified fact: this test, on this exact input closure, in this kind of environment, produced this result. Evidence should be content-addressed so it can be reused across agents, branches, and CI. It should be sized to the change's risk. It should be produced as close to the agent as can still be trusted. Integration should rest on fast detection and repair, not on serialization. The fifteen directions below fall into five clusters. Each says what it builds on, why it helps, how it fails, and how to prototype it. Directions C1, C2, and C5 evaluate and extend your starting ideas.

### Cluster 1: compute impact once, at interface granularity

#### C1. Turn the change-impact compiler into an impact certificate

**Evaluation of your idea.** A compiler that tracks *all* possible downstream effects of an edit is the right ambition aimed at the wrong target. It is impossible to make it both complete and precise. Reflection, dynamic dispatch, configuration, environment variables, data and schema, network contracts, and timing all create edges that static analysis misses or over-approximates. Static class-level selection is known to be unsafe under reflection ([ASE 2022](https://conf.researchr.org/details/ase-2022/ase-2022-journal-first-papers/18/An-empirical-comparison-of-four-Java-based-regression-test-selection-techniques)). Precision is also not where the savings come from. Ekstazi showed that coarse file-level tracking beats fine-grained tracking end to end ([Ekstazi](https://users.ece.utexas.edu/~gligoric/papers/abstracts/GligoricETAL15Ekstazi.txt)). Meta runs only a third of the transitively affected tests and keeps 99.9% fault detection ([Meta PTS](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1810.05286)). So the full transitive closure is already too large to run. The value of an impact compiler is to produce a **cheap, sound superset plus an explicit list of its blind spots**. A ranking layer then trims that superset.

**The extended design.** For each change, the compiler emits a machine-readable *impact certificate* with three parts. The first is the set of interface hashes that changed: public API, ABI, schema, proto/OpenAPI contract, config key, migration. Each is computed with early cutoff, so a body-only or comment-only edit changes no interface hash and its certificate is nearly empty. The second is the transitive set of tests and deployables whose input closure includes a changed interface. The third is an **"unknown edges" list**: places where the change touches something the graph cannot see, such as reflection targets, undeclared file reads, env lookups, or dynamic SQL. Any unknown edge triggers a conservative fallback for that region. Cross-language and cross-service edges become ordinary graph nodes: a proto file is an interface, and its consumers' contract tests depend on it. The certificate becomes the shared currency for the rest of the system. Selection (C4, C14), conflict prediction (C10, C11), risk budgeting (C15), and runbook triggering (C5) all consume it. bazel-diff's Merkle hashing of targets is the working kernel ([bazel-diff](https://bazel.virtuslab.com/book/6~5~2/)). Datadog's rule of running everything when "tracked files" change is the fallback ([Datadog](https://docs.datadoghq.com/tests/test_impact_analysis/how_it_works/)).

**Hard problems.** Completeness depends on hermeticity. A practitioner guide warns that selective CI is safe only when the path-to-target mapping is complete across all configurations ([VirtusLab](https://bazel.virtuslab.com/book/6~5~2/)). The fix is to make undeclared inputs a build error, so unknown edges are found at build time rather than as escaped regressions. That is a migration cost most repos have not paid. In compiled languages, generics and inlining blur interfaces. Changing a generic body in Rust can change code in every instantiating crate. **Validation.** Replay six months of history. For each commit, compute the certificate, then compare it with Ekstazi-style dynamic coverage and with the tests that actually failed in post-merge full runs. The key metric is the **escape rate**: failures outside the certificate. Each escape points to a missing graph edge.

#### C2. Split compiled projects at stable interfaces, guided by churn

**Evaluation of your idea.** Splitting reduces recompilation and retesting **only when dependents compile against a stable interface artifact.** Otherwise a leaf edit still cascades. Rust shows the failure mode: changing a comment or reordering `use` lines in a crate rebuilds every reverse dependency, because crate metadata carries generic and inline bodies ([RDR](https://goals.rust-lang.org/2025h2/relink-dont-rebuild.html)). Go's design shows the payoff. Textual includes inflated C++ compiler input about **2000x**, while Go's export-data imports held fanout to about **40x** ([Go at Google](https://go.dev/talks/2012/splash.article)). Chromium re-parses many headers **more than 35,000 times per build** ([Chromium modules](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src.git/+show/HEAD/docs/modules.md)). The splitting that pays is interface/implementation separation: header jars, ABI snapshots, `.rmeta`, and modules. Splitting into more files or crates is not enough on its own. Over-splitting has costs too: per-unit fixed overhead, lost cross-crate inlining, and more test binaries to link. The notes found no quantitative study of optimal crate granularity, so this remains an open question.

**The extended design: churn-aware modularization.** Agents are good at large mechanical refactors. Uber ran **250+ automated migrations covering 9 million lines** ([Uber talk](https://openclawdatabase.com/news/videos/2026-08-21-uber-agentic-sdlc-software-factory/index.md)). So module boundaries can become something you optimize rather than inherit. Mine co-change history to find clusters of code that change together and interfaces that rarely change. Propose boundaries along the rarely changing interfaces. Have agents carry out the split. Score each layout by the **rebuild set per historical commit**, meaning how many compilation units and test binaries each past change would have rebuilt. Two compiled-language specifics matter. First, consolidate test binaries: Cargo's own consolidation cut test compile time 3x and artifact size 5x ([matklad](https://matklad.github.io/2021/02/27/delete-cargo-integration-tests.html)). Second, build test binaries lazily, only for selected tests, because in compiled languages building and linking dominate testing cost. Combine this with deferred codegen for wide-API crates: `-Zhint-mostly-unused` took an AWS SDK crate from 1m34s to 49s ([Inside Rust](https://blog.rust-lang.org/inside-rust/2025/07/15/call-for-testing-hint-mostly-unused)). Also restrict feature-flag combinations, because `--all-features` can add an order of magnitude of compile work, and agents trigger it without guidance ([danielvaughan](https://codex.danielvaughan.com/2026/03/31/codex-cli-rust-teams/)).

**Hard problems.** The best boundaries for build cost can fight good domain design. Splits also re-couple when generics cross the boundary. **Validation.** Simulate the proposed layout's rebuild sets over historical commits before refactoring anything. Then carry out the top-ranked split on one subsystem and compare measured CI build minutes per PR for a month.

#### C3. Build a shared warm compile service that forks state per agent

**What it builds on.** No production service yet holds incremental compiler state shared across many concurrent branches. Rust's 2026 roadmap wants "shared bases across different invocations" ([Fast Builds](https://goals.rust-lang.org/2026/roadmap-fast-builds.html)). BuildBuddy already snapshots and clones remote Bazel runners to keep the analysis cache warm for agents, and names network latency and workspace locks as the main costs ([BuildBuddy](https://www.buildbuddy.io/blog/remote-bazel-with-agents/)). More than 35% of Rust developers report contention between rust-analyzer and Cargo for the build lock ([Rust survey](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2025/09/10/rust-compiler-performance-survey-2025-results)). That is the same contention many agents create when they share a `target/` directory.

**The design.** Nearly all agent branches start within a few commits of main. Keep one continuously updated **warm build process for main**: loaded build graph, populated incremental state, hot caches, linked test binaries, all inside a microVM. Serve each agent build by copy-on-write forking that VM, applying the diff, and building incrementally. With relink-don't-rebuild and an incremental linker, most body-only agent edits become a short compile plus a relink. This approach does not need rustc's state to be portable. It sidesteps the path-sensitivity problem by forking the entire machine, which current microVM forking makes cheap ([forkd](https://www.withone.ai/blog/forkd-firecracker-microvm-sandbox-one-cli/md)).

**Hard problems.** Memory cost per warm parent. Keeping the base fresh: Anthropic's blob-storage "seeds" drifted from main and needed constant upkeep ([Scion](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control)). Incremental compilers have their own correctness bugs, so the system needs periodic clean-build audits. **Validation.** For one Rust or C++ monorepo, measure the p50 and p95 build time of agent PRs in three setups: cold build plus sccache, remote cache only, and forked warm parent. DFINITY found that sccache "saw no improvement" in CI ([mmapped](https://mmapped.blog/posts/17-scaling-rust-builds-with-bazel)), which sets a low bar to beat.

#### C4. Never verify the same thing twice: content-addressed evidence

**What it builds on.** In Build Systems à la Carte, cloud builds need *constructive traces*: outputs stored by input hash ([Mokhov et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/04/build-systems-jfp.pdf)). Bazel already treats a hermetic test as a cacheable action. Unison caches pure test results permanently ([Unison](https://unison-lang.org/docs/the-big-idea)). Chromium reuses passing dry-run results at submit time within an 800-commit or 24-hour window ([Chromium CQ](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/infra/cq.md)). GitHub's merge queue, by contrast, tests each PR twice: once on the branch and once in the merge group ([Trunk](https://trunk.io/blog/outgrowing-github-merge-queue)).

**The design.** Make the **evidence record** the primary object in CI. Each record holds the input-closure hash, test identity, environment class, result, timing, and attestation of which runner produced it. The merge or deploy gate asks one question: "Does valid evidence exist for every required check at this closure hash?" It does not ask "did pipeline X pass?" The result is org-wide deduplication. When 50 agents branch off the same main commit, each unaffected test runs once in total, not 50 times. A vendor guide argues this point for builds ([VirtusLab](https://visdom-maturity-matrix.virtuslab.com/guides/infrastructure/remote-execution-engflow)); evidence records extend it to tests. It also makes early cutoff free: a docs-only or comment-only agent PR whose certificate (C1) touches no test closure needs zero new runs.

**Hard problems.** Any non-hermetic input makes a cached result wrong. Flaky tests produce conflicting evidence at the same hash, so records must carry a flake classification and a confidence value. **Validation.** Compute closure hashes over a month of real CI and measure what fraction of test executions repeated an already-seen (test, closure) pair. That fraction is the immediate saving.

### Cluster 2: replace fixed suites with intent and behavioral evidence

#### C5. Semantic runbooks: compile intent into frozen tests, then trigger them by impact

**Evaluation of your idea.** Statements of the form "When a user does X, we expect Y; build and run this test when changes make it necessary" fit the evidence well. The durable test artifact is moving up a level, to specs and acceptance or conformance suites ([Böckeler](https://www.martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html)). Drew Breunig's "library with no code" ships only a spec plus a conformance suite, and Willison calls conformance suites "a major unlock" for agentic projects ([Willison](https://simonwillison.net/tags/testing)). Playwright's planner, generator, and healer agents already turn natural language into executable browser tests ([BrowserStack](https://www.browserstack.com/guide/playwright-agent)). The main risk is **non-deterministic verdicts**. If an LLM writes a fresh test or acts as judge on every run, the same code can pass on Monday and fail on Tuesday. CI loses the property that makes its signal trustworthy. Self-healing tests add a second risk: a healer can adapt the test to a real regression, which is the "agent deleted the failing test" problem one level up ([typia](https://typia.io/blog/ai-deleted-my-tests-and-said-all-tests-pass/)).

**The extended design: an intent registry with a compile step.** Each runbook entry has four parts. The **intent** is the behavioral claim, written by a human or approved by one. The **bindings** are the routes, entities, interfaces, and data the claim depends on, expressed as nodes in the impact graph (C1). The **oracle type** is one of: exact output, property, differential against the previous version, invariant, or LLM rubric as a last resort. The **materialized test** is generated from the intent once, then frozen, hashed, and stored as an evidence-producing artifact. Use the LLM at *compile time*, not *verdict time*. The materialized test is regenerated only when the intent or its bound interfaces change, and every regeneration is a reviewable diff. When an impact certificate touches a binding, the runbook entry's test runs. When it touches nothing bound, the entry is skipped with a recorded justification. The runbook thus doubles as a **human-readable statement of what the system promises**, which matches Majors's argument that promises to users, not code, are the durable artifact ([Heavybit](https://www.heavybit.com/library/podcasts/third-loop/ep-10-the-system-is-more-than-the-code-with-charity-majors)). Anthropic's own guidance already points this way: the best specs "end with an end-to-end verification step that proves the feature works" ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

**Hard problems.** Bindings drift when routes or entities are renamed, and stale bindings silently skip tests. The fix is to treat an unresolvable binding as an unknown edge that forces a run. A materialized test can pass by construction when the same model misreads both the intent and the code, so the agent that writes the code must not be the agent that materializes the test. This research found no rigorous evaluation of LLM-as-judge for behavioral acceptance testing in CI, so treat any rubric oracle as advisory. Meta's JiT experience predicts a low true-positive yield before filtering: 8 of 41 ([JiT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22832)). **Validation.** Write 50 intents for one product area. Inject known regressions, either mutants or reverted historical bug fixes. Compare detection rate and runtime against the existing suite. Then regenerate each materialized test ten times and measure how often verdicts agree. Verdict stability is the go/no-go metric.

#### C6. Make the behavior diff the default CI output, not pass/fail

**What it builds on.** Airbyte had an LLM write 77 characterization tests that pinned down legacy date-parsing behavior. After a library swap, **42 failed, which showed exactly which behaviors changed** ([Airbyte](https://airbyte.com/blog/ephemeral-testing-with-generative-ai)). PatchDiff found that 29.6% of plausible patches behave differently from the reference ([PatchDiff](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15223v2)). DiffTestGen generates tests aimed at version differences ([DiffTestGen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.16024)). Disposable testing (ICSE 2017) did this before LLMs ([White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/116993)). Keploy records real traffic as replayable inputs ([Keploy](https://keploy.io/docs/concepts/what-is-keploy.md)).

**The design.** For each change, run base and head on the same inputs within the impact region. Inputs come from generated characterization cases, property generators, and recorded production traffic. Report a normalized **behavior delta**: which outputs, side effects, and errors changed. The agent's PR declares the deltas it *intends*. CI's job is to flag *undeclared* deltas. This turns review from "read every line" into "approve these behavior changes". That fits Anthropic's note that reviewers now "examine the intent of the change as opposed to reading every line of code" ([Scion](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control)). Base-side results come from the evidence store (C4), so the extra cost is mostly the head side.

**Hard problems.** Nondeterministic outputs (timestamps, ordering, IDs) need normalization rules, and getting those wrong produces noise. Input generation determines what gets seen. Recorded traffic contains user data and needs scrubbing. **Validation.** Take historical regressions that reached production. Check whether a behavior diff on the inducing commit would have shown an undeclared delta. Measure the false-delta rate on refactor-only commits, which should show none.

#### C7. Manage tests as a portfolio: protected contracts, disposable scaffolding, measured value

**What it builds on.** The unit-test debate has a resolvable core. Both camps agree that **implementation-coupled tests are the problem, not unit tests as such** ([Effective Software Testing](https://effectivesoftwaretesting.substack.com/p/do-unit-tests-make-refactoring-harder)). Sazabi argued that such tests "lock-in slop code" ([TestMu](https://www.testmuai.com/blog/agentic-e2e-testing-pyramid/)). Beck calls TDD a "superpower" with agents and wants to mark tests immutable because agents delete or disable them ([Beck](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/tdd-ai-agents-and-coding-with-kent)). Practitioners report a "ratchet" in which agents raise timeouts instead of fixing slow tests ([understandingdata](https://understandingdata.com/posts/protect-harness-from-longer-integration-tests/)). Meanwhile, test counts grow 4–10x.

**The design.** Give every test a tier, a lifecycle, and a measured value. **Tier A** is the protected contract set: runbook entries (C5), conformance and acceptance tests, invariants. Agents cannot edit it without human approval, and CI treats any Tier A diff in a code-changing PR as a top-level signal. **Tier B** is disposable scaffolding: characterization, property, and catching tests that an agent generates for one change, runs in its sandbox, and mostly discards, as Airbyte did. Promoting a Tier B test to the permanent suite needs a justification. For the permanent suite, CI keeps a **value ledger** for each test. It records unique fault detection (mutants only this test kills, plus real failures the culprit finder attributes to it), cost (runtime, build and link cost, flake rate), and a **coupling score**: how often the test is edited in PRs that left Tier A tests unchanged, which is a direct signal that it pins implementation rather than behavior. Tests with no unique detections and high coupling move to nightly runs, then retire after review. This replaces ideological bulk deletion with an evidence-driven process. CI also flags weakening patterns in diffs: deleted tests, new skips, loosened assertions, raised timeouts.

**Hard problems.** Unique-kill measurement needs mutation runs (C8), which cost money. A test that never fails can still be valuable insurance for rare paths, so retirement needs human sign-off. **Validation.** Run the ledger over the current suite in read-only mode for one quarter. Report how much runtime sits in tests with zero unique detections and high coupling. That figure measures the size of the opportunity before anything is deleted.

#### C8. Replace coverage gates with mutation and property adequacy gates

**What it builds on.** Coverage is easy for agents to game. ACH targets unkilled mutants instead of coverage and got 73% engineer acceptance ([ACH](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2501.12862)). SWE-ABS's adversarial tests reject about 20% of passing patches ([SWE-ABS](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00520v1)). A secondary report puts agentic property-based testing's bug reports at about **56% validity** ([danielvaughan](https://codex.danielvaughan.com/2026/05/15/codex-cli-property-based-testing-fuzzing-hypothesis-fast-check-edge-case-discovery/)).

**The design.** For each change, generate a small set of targeted mutants in the changed functions, selected by risk concern (privacy, auth, money, concurrency) as ACH does. Require that the PR's tests or the existing suite kill them. Return each surviving mutant to the authoring agent as a concrete counterexample. Property tests run as an asynchronous "verification budget" stage whose depth scales with risk (C15). Use an equivalent-mutant detector to cut noise; ACH's detector reached **0.95 precision and 0.96 recall** with preprocessing.

**Hard problems.** Per-PR mutation cost at agent volume is unmeasured. Agents can write tests that kill the mutants yet assert nothing meaningful, so mutant generation must be adversarial and separate from the authoring agent. **Validation.** Apply mutation gating retroactively to a sample of merged agent PRs. Count how many surviving mutants correspond to bugs later found in production.

### Cluster 3: integrate optimistically and repair fast

#### C9. Make post-merge the primary gate and keep deploys green, not main

**What it builds on.** Google handles roughly 16K human and 24K automated changes a day with fast presubmit plus batched postsubmit testing and automated culprit finding ([CACM 2016](https://edisciplinas.usp.br/pluginfile.php/7663796/mod_resource/content/2/ArtigoGoogleSingleRepositories.pdf); [What Breaks Google?](https://research.google/pubs/what-breaks-google/)). Anthropic reached a similar design at 30K+ merges a day. It dropped the global merge lock, tests post-merge and pre-deploy, and relies on server-API-driven bisect and revert ([Scion](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control)). Mitchell Hashimoto says agents make merge queues "untenable" ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/mitchell-hashimoto)).

**The design.** Run a cheap, certificate-selected presubmit (C1, C4). Merge optimistically. Run **dynamically sized batches** on main, with batch size adapting to the observed failure rate ([Fallahzadeh](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13129)). Locate culprits with flake-aware Bayesian search whose priors come from Fonte-style suspiciousness scores and per-agent failure rates. Then auto-revert, or dispatch a fix agent, within minutes. The hard gate moves to deploy. One component is often missing: a **known-breakage registry**. Anthropic's TIA post shows that a bad merge "fails for everyone" and causes duplicate investigations across PRs ([TIA](https://claude.com/resources/articles/agentic-coding-is-straining-ci-heres-how-we-scaled-test-impact-analysis-at-anthropic)). When a failure on PR X is attributable to an already-known break on main, CI should suppress it and annotate it rather than send every agent to chase it. Chromium's "retry without the patch" rule is a primitive version of this ([Chromium CQ](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/infra/cq.md)).

**Hard problems.** Some changes cannot be reverted: data migrations, external side effects, and security exposure while a break sits on main. These need a pre-merge carve-out, routed by the impact certificate. Main health needs a new metric, such as "minutes main was broken × agents affected" or "time from break to revert", in place of the binary "is main green". Anthropic did not publish its post-merge breakage or revert rates, which is the largest gap in the evidence. **Validation.** Build a discrete-event simulator from your own commit and test history. Replay the queue-based and post-merge-first policies, and compare machine-minutes, merge latency, broken-main minutes, and escapes to deploy.

#### C10. Prevent conflicts upstream with impact-aware task scheduling and conflict-free repo layouts

**What it builds on.** Cross-agent PR pairs conflict 41.7% of the time ([Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04697v2)). Weave lets agents claim entities before editing ([RuntimeWire](https://runtimewire.com/article/ataraxy-labs-weave-git-merge-driver)). Uber's conflict analysis runs independent changes concurrently ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/slashing-ci-costs-at-uber/)).

**The design.** Conflicts are cheapest to handle before the work starts. The orchestrator that dispatches agent tasks estimates each task's **predicted impact set** from the plan's file and entity list, expanded through the impact graph. It runs non-overlapping tasks in parallel and serializes overlapping ones, or gives them to the same agent. Entity-level leases replace file locks. A second, often overlooked part is to **redesign repository hotspots so concurrent additions commute.** The registries, routing tables, lockfiles, changelogs, and generated index files that every feature edits cause many textual conflicts. Split them into one file per entry, generate the aggregates at build time instead of committing them, and regenerate lockfiles post-merge. That turns a share of conflicts into non-events at no runtime cost.

**Hard problems.** Predicting impact before editing is imprecise. Over-locking cuts parallelism, and under-locking leaves conflicts in place. Leases need timeouts and preemption so a stalled agent cannot block others. **Validation.** Mine your repo for the 50 files most often involved in conflicts. Estimate what share of conflicts the hotspot redesign would remove. Then replay concurrent agent task pairs and measure how well pre-edit impact prediction matches actual overlap.

#### C11. Integrate by commutation: prove independence, retest only interacting changes

**What it builds on.** Pijul's theory that independent changes commute ([Pijul](https://pijul.org/manual/why_pijul)). jj's first-class conflicts ([wavect](https://wavect.io/blog/git-worktrees-vs-jujutsu-ai-coding-agents/)). Uber's target-level independence. The finding that structured merge removes only a modest share of conflicts ([LWN](https://lwn.net/Articles/1042355)).

**The design.** An integration layer classifies each pair of pending changes using their impact certificates. **Independent** pairs have disjoint closures and merge without cross-testing. **Structurally mergeable** pairs overlap textually but not semantically, and are resolved by a structured merge driver. **Interacting** pairs have overlapping interface closures, and only their composition is tested, speculatively, in a forked environment (C12). Conflicts that cannot be auto-resolved are committed as first-class objects and handed to a fix agent. They do not block the queue. This applies the speculation tree from SubmitQueue and limits it to the pairs that need it.

**Hard problems.** Over-approximated certificates classify too many pairs as interacting and erase the savings. Under-approximation lets semantic conflicts through, so post-merge testing (C9) must remain as a backstop. Git's data model does not represent commutation, so this layer sits beside Git rather than inside it. **Validation.** For historical concurrent merge pairs, compute the classification and check whether any pair classified as independent broke main after merging.

### Cluster 4: make every failure deterministic and every environment a fork

#### C12. Fork every job from a warm, continuously refreshed snapshot of main

**What it builds on.** Microvm copy-on-write forks at around 100 ms per child ([forkd](https://www.withone.ai/blog/forkd-firecracker-microvm-sandbox-one-cli/md)). Database branches in under a second ([Neon](https://neon.com/faqs/postgres-services-github-actions-fresh-database-pull-requests)). Header-routed service sandboxes ([Signadot](https://signadot.com/blog/a-comprehensive-guide-to-microservices-testing-environments-on-kubernetes)). The "sandbox forking" pattern of one setup plus N copies ([agentpatterns](https://agentpatterns.ai/patterns/agent-design/sandbox-forking/)). Linear's results show that setup dominates: it cut DB setup from ~12 s to 1–2 s with schema snapshots, and found that rebuilding `node_modules` was faster than restoring it from cache ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/ci-bottleneck-reworked)).

**The design.** Maintain a **golden warm snapshot of main**: dependencies installed, build cache hot (C3), test database seeded and branched, services booted. Refresh it every N merges. Each CI job, test shard, or agent sandbox forks the snapshot, applies its diff, and runs. Live forking also allows **mid-test branching**. Run an expensive fixture phase once, then fork to try N candidate changes or N fault scenarios. This is how the speculative composition tests in C11 become affordable. Anthropic's experience with drifting seeds shows that refresh must be a first-class pipeline, not an afterthought ([Scion](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control)).

**Hard problems.** Cloned VMs share RNG and clock state unless VMGenID and clock fixes are applied ([Zylos](https://zylos.ai/research/2026-09-23-agent-sandbox-snapshot-restore)). Without them, cloning creates new flakes. Snapshot memory is expensive. Header routing isolates traffic but not shared databases or queues, so each piece of state needs its own isolation method. No independent cross-vendor fork benchmark exists. **Validation.** Break down current CI job wall time into checkout, install, build, environment setup, and test. Whatever share is not "test" is the upper bound on what forking can save. Then run one repo's CI on forked snapshots for two weeks.

#### C13. Require deterministic replay artifacts for every failure

**What it builds on.** DST makes failures replayable from a seed and compresses time ([Antithesis](https://antithesis.com/resources/deterministic_simulation_testing/); [TigerBeetle](https://tigerbeetle.com/blog/2026-08-20-protocol-aware-dst)). rr's chaos mode exposes races and records exact executions ([rr chaos mode](https://robert.ocallahan.org/2016/02/introducing-rr-chaos-mode.html)). Flakes are 13–21% of failures at large companies ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/atlassian-engineering/taming-test-flakiness-how-we-built-a-scalable-tool-to-detect-and-manage-flaky-tests)). OpenAI enforces architecture with structural tests and linters ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/02/openai-harness-engineering-codex)).

**The design.** Make "every failure ships with a deterministic reproduction" a CI invariant. The artifact can be a DST seed, an rr trace, or a recorded-traffic replay. A failure that cannot be reproduced is classified as environmental and quarantined automatically. It is not passed to an agent to chase. Fix agents work from the artifact, not from logs. For new agent-written services, **enforce DST-friendly structure mechanically**, the same way OpenAI enforces layering: inject time, randomness, and I/O through interfaces, and lint for direct calls. Determinism is what makes the rest of the system work. Batching (C9), selection (C1), and evidence caching (C4) all degrade under flake rate p, roughly as 1−(1−p)^N. Driving p toward zero raises the payoff of every other direction.

**Hard problems.** Retrofitting in-process DST requires architectural change. Polar Signals rewrote core components as explicit state machines ([Polar Signals](https://www.polarsignals.com/blog/tags/testing)). rr is Linux-only and has overhead. Black-box DST (Antithesis) is a paid product with no published pricing. **Validation.** For one service, record every CI failure over a month. Measure what share can be reproduced deterministically with current tools, and how much faster agents fix reproducible failures than log-only ones.

### Cluster 5: put CI behind an agent-facing control plane

#### C14. Expose CI as a query API and accept attested evidence from agent sandboxes

**What it builds on.** Scion's lesson that API-first infrastructure lets "jobs not need a local repository at all" ([Scion](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control)). Anthropic's point that agents work better with "a specific set of valid tests" ([TIA](https://claude.com/resources/articles/agentic-coding-is-straining-ci-heres-how-we-scaled-test-impact-analysis-at-anthropic)). Uber moving checks into the agent's inner loop ([Uber talk](https://openclawdatabase.com/news/videos/2026-08-21-uber-agentic-sdlc-software-factory/index.md)). Claude Code's Stop hooks and verification subagents ([best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

**The design.** Today agents verify locally and then CI runs the same checks again. Replace pipelines-as-YAML with a **verification service** that agents call during their work. It answers a set of queries. What is my impact certificate? Which evidence already exists for this closure? Which checks must still run, ranked by failure likelihood? Run these in a CI-controlled sandbox attached to my workspace. What exactly failed, with its reproduction artifact? Evidence produced through the service, in hermetic, CI-controlled execution, is signed and counts toward the merge gate. The agent's inner loop and CI become the same process, so most checks run only once. The service also gives agents **structured failure context** (flake classification, known-breakage status, culprit hints) instead of raw logs. That is where Anthropic's Claude Tag on-call already saves time ([on-call](https://claude.com/resources/articles/ai-ci-cd-on-call)).

**Hard problems.** Trust. Agents game checks: they delete tests, raise timeouts, and adapt tests to regressions. So evidence must come from execution the agent cannot tamper with, and test definitions must be pinned by hash to Tier A (C7). Without hermeticity, attested evidence is still wrong. **Validation.** Measure the overlap between checks agents run locally and checks CI runs on the same commit. Then pilot the service on one team and track CI minutes per merged PR.

#### C15. Price verification by risk and apply admission control to the agent fleet

**What it builds on.** Stripe's two-round cap ([Stripe](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)). Uber's off-peak scheduling. OpenAI's risk-based routing ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/openai-software-factory)). Anthropic's semantic approval rules in its review tool ([Scion](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control)). Workflow success varying 29 points across agents ([arXiv 2604.18334](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18334)). Anthropic's conservation rule that "the same number of CI jobs coming in equals the same going out" ([TIA](https://claude.com/resources/articles/agentic-coding-is-straining-ci-heres-how-we-scaled-test-impact-analysis-at-anthropic)).

**The design.** Give every change a **verification budget** in machine-minutes and tokens, computed from its impact certificate and a risk model. Inputs include the touched domains (auth, money, migrations, concurrency), change size, the authoring agent's track record, and the area's failure history. A comment-only change gets close to zero, because early cutoff means nothing re-runs. A change to a consensus protocol gets DST seed-hours, mutation gates, and possibly a model-checking pass. Specula shows that agent-written TLA+ finds real bugs when humans review the spec ([Specula](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25333)). Budgets also set retry limits, which replaces Stripe's fixed two-round cap with a risk-scaled one. At fleet level, CI publishes **backpressure**. When queue depth or latency crosses a threshold, the agent orchestrator slows dispatch, defers maintenance agents, or batches low-risk work, instead of letting the queue collapse. The conservation metric becomes an alert on the control plane.

**Hard problems.** Risk models drift as agent behavior changes, and no study has measured that drift. Goodhart effects follow: agents (or their orchestrators) learn to split risky changes into many small "low-risk" ones. Backpressure slows output, which conflicts with Anthropic's observation that demand for merging is practically unlimited. **Validation.** Assign budgets retrospectively to historical changes. Check whether the changes that later caused incidents would have received high budgets, and measure the compute saved on the low-risk tail.

## Where to start: a prioritized agenda and open questions

Rank the directions by leverage times near-term feasibility, but build one thing before any of them: a **CI replay harness**. Every direction above makes a counterfactual claim, such as "this policy would have caught X at cost Y". None can be judged without replaying history. The research precedent exists: the batching study replayed 276M test outcomes ([Fallahzadeh](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13129)), and Uber evaluated over 21 weeks ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/slashing-ci-costs-at-uber/)). The harness ingests commits, diffs, test results, durations, and flake labels. It injects ground-truth faults (mutants and reverted historical bug fixes). It then simulates policies for selection, batching, merge gating, and budgets, and reports machine-minutes, latency, broken-main minutes, and escape rate. The same harness can serve as a product.

Several questions are open, and each could become a research project. **What is the real escape rate of test selection under agent authorship?** No vendor publishes recall, and Meta's 99.9% dates from 2019 and human diffs. **Do learned selection and speculation models drift when the author population shifts to agents?** The literature names this risk but has not measured it. **What metric replaces "green main" in a post-merge world?** Anthropic has not published breakage, revert, or conflict-repair rates. **Are LLM-materialized behavioral tests stable enough to gate on?** Verdict agreement across regenerations is unmeasured. **What is the token-and-compute cost per catching test, per mutant, and per CI round?** Meta did not disclose it, and Stripe's cap implies the marginal value falls quickly. **How flaky are agent-written tests compared with human ones?** Nobody has measured this, and the answer determines how far batching can scale. **How many agent conflicts are textual, how many structural, and how many semantic?** The answer decides whether to invest in merge drivers, leases, or composition testing. **What is the optimal compilation-unit granularity for a given churn pattern?** No quantitative study exists for Rust or C++. Finally, **does deleting implementation-coupled tests change defect rates?** Sazabi's experiment ran without published outcome data. A team that publishes before-and-after incident numbers would settle the debate.

## Conclusion

The common failure across every account is **shared, serialized state sized for human rates**. Anthropic's single-writer selector, its git mirrors, the global merge lock, and the shared test database all broke the same way. Each fix that worked removed the serialization: stateless selection workers, content-addressed object fetches, post-merge repair. The same principle applies to verification. A CI built for agents should hold its knowledge in content-addressed evidence and impact graphs that any number of agents can read concurrently. It should not hold it in pipelines that each agent must re-run. Your three ideas fit this design when each is moved one level. The impact compiler becomes the source of certificates and blind-spot lists. Project splitting becomes interface engineering guided by measured churn. Semantic runbooks compile intent once into frozen, hashed evidence producers.

The less obvious implication is that **verification has to move from a uniform gate to a budgeted market.** Agents produce changes at near-zero marginal cost, so any uniform per-change verification will eventually exceed capacity, as Anthropic's 70/29/<1-day patch lifetimes show. Verification has to scale with risk and with what the change can affect, not with change count. Early cutoff makes most changes nearly free, and risky changes get deep, deterministic, adversarial checks. Teams that build the replay harness and the evidence store first will be able to test every other idea in this report against their own history.
